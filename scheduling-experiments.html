Scheduling Experiments (2026)

This page reports the experiments behind the leapfrogging, idle and pinning policies of Lace, and the reasons for each choice. Lace was compared with OpenCilk 3.0 and with the scheduler of ParlayLib on several machines, and every mechanism had to show a measured benefit to stay in.

Goals and constraints

  • Fast: at least as fast as OpenCilk and Parlay on fork-join programs.

  • Quiet when idle: an idle Lace uses almost no CPU, so that a program that uses Lace now and then does not pay for it in between.

  • Responsive when needed: a parallel region that starts after an idle period starts quickly.

  • Do no harm: Lace is used from other programs and languages (Sylvan, bindings for Rust, Java and Python), and must not slow them down: no steal storms, no cores taken from other programs, nothing that breaks under interference.

  • The calling thread is not a worker. This is by design: it keeps the API simple and makes Lace easy to call from other languages. OpenCilk and Parlay make the calling thread a worker; Lace instead makes the handoff to a worker fast.

  • Keep it simple. A mechanism stays only if it shows a clear benefit on some platform and some measure (throughput, idle CPU, latency, storms, interference); a constant is preferred over an adaptive mechanism that gives nothing on the hardware tested.

Method

All runs were on Linux, on shared nodes of a SLURM cluster (except on the desktop processor), with 36 hardware threads (18 cores) per job and 16 workers unless stated otherwise.

Systems and benchmarks

Every system was compiled with the same compiler (the clang 19.1.7 of OpenCilk 3.0), so that only the scheduler differs. The references are OpenCilk 3.0, the Parlay scheduler, and Lace v2.4.0.

  • The Lace benchmark suite (18 programs: fib, uts T1L/T2L/T3L, nqueens, dfs, matmul, cholesky, integrate, heat, cilksort, fft, knapsack, lu, pi, quicksort, rectmul, strassen; uts T3XXL in some runs), and OpenCilk ports of the same programs. Nine of them print a checksum of their result, which agreed between all variants and worker counts. uts T3L is a stress test, not a target: its binomial tree gives every unexplored node the same expected work, so that stealing the oldest task gets no more than any other, as its authors intended (“It is indeed our goal to construct a benchmark that in its most challenging form challenges all load balancing strategies”, Prins, UNC 2003).

  • PBBS v2 (up to 67 inputs of 21 benchmarks) through the Parlay scheduler plugin, against Parlay’s own scheduler and OpenCilk.

  • Sylvan (BDD and LDD operations on BEEM models), the main real user of Lace, as the do-no-harm test.

  • Micro benchmarks for the idle and external-call behaviour: short calls from outside Lace, a tiny region 200 µs and 2 ms after the previous one, a region of 1000 tasks of 1 µs after a 5 ms sleep, regions of 10000 and 100000 tasks of 100 ns, steal storms (one or four chains of steps that fork two empty or 1 µs tasks, so that at most two or eight workers have work), and the CPU an idle Lace uses in 2 s after work.

  • Interference: busy-loop processes on 4 of the 16 cores of a run, and two copies of a benchmark at the same time on twice the cores.

Ablation

The candidate design was tested by leave-one-out (remove one mechanism) and add-one (add an alternative or change a value), in rounds, until no single change made it better.

  • One binary for all configurations. Code layout alone moves results by 3-6% between builds, more than most single mechanisms. An ablation build (LACE_TUNABLE) compiles in every mechanism and reads its setting from the environment, so all configurations run the same code. Normal builds were checked to have the same machine code as without these switches, and the final code, from which the switches are removed, the same machine code as the experiment code with the final settings (bar one redundant check).

  • The same environment size for every configuration. The size of the environment moves the initial stack, which can change results by itself (Mytkowicz et al., ASPLOS 2009). Every run pads its environment to the same size, with a different size per round.

  • Configurations run interleaved, in a shuffled or rotating order, 3-5 rounds; results are geomeans over benchmarks of the median time per configuration.

  • The statistics options cost nothing measurable. Builds with LACE_PIE_TIMES, LACE_COUNT_TASKS, LACE_COUNT_STEALS or LACE_COUNT_SPLITS, alone or all together, ran the suite within 0.6% of the plain build at 16 workers, and fib, knapsack and uts T2L within 0.6% at one worker (LACE_COUNT_TASKS: knapsack 1.4%), and seven PBBS benchmarks within 0.3%. Only nqueens moved more, 5-10%, also between builds that differ only in an unrelated counter: code layout. PIE times can therefore explain a gap without causing one.

Pitfalls that cost time and are worth knowing:

  • Runs under perf stat with many multiplexed events made Lace look 4-9% slower than OpenCilk; without perf the difference was 0-3%. Lace does tens of thousands of context switches, which perf makes expensive.

  • Comparing separately compiled variants confused code layout with policy: an 11% “pinning gain” on PBBS came mostly from two different builds, and in a storm of empty tasks on Intel the final build took 12 ns per step, the same code in the ablation binary 16.

  • Shared nodes have transient anomalies (once, unpinned fib ran 3-4 times slower in two rounds, never again in 240 runs), and heat is bimodal on some allocations (1.8 or 2.5 s, whatever the configuration).

Leapfrogging

A worker that waits at a join for a stolen task leapfrogs: it steals from the thief, since that work is part of the awaited task.

Random leaps removed. Since 2014, a leapfrogging worker also stole a random task after 32 failed attempts. That takes the victim’s oldest and largest task, and the join stays blocked under it until that task is done; in phased algorithms (cholesky, matmul, lu) the next phase waits behind that join. Random steals also beget steals and move data between caches and sockets (cholesky: 349k instead of 60k steals, 7x the remote cache traffic).

Transitive leaps. Without random leaps, uts T3L (a tree with long sequential chains) waited at joins 18% of the time. With transitive leaps, a waiting worker whose thief has nothing to steal steals from the worker that the thief waits for, down the chain. Each worker publishes whom it waits for in a cache line that a steal attempt reads anyway, and only after a first failed attempt (most waits end before that). Without transitive leaps uts T3L takes 1.22-1.26 times as long on all three machines. How far the chain is followed (1, 4 or 16 hops) and whether the target is published at once made no difference; the design uses 4 hops and publishes lazily.

Steal storms

When there are many idle workers and only small tasks, idle workers keep succeeding at stealing worthless tasks, so they never back off, and every stolen task turns the owner’s join into a remote one. Lace v2.0.3 (before the backoff) took 1296 ns per step for a chain of empty tasks at 16 workers, v2.4.0 72 ns, and the code before this work still kept all 16 cores busy.

A steal of a tiny task counts as a failed steal for the backoff: thieves then back off and leave the work to its owner. The thief times only the stolen task, not the steal.

The threshold adapts to the machine. A stolen task counts as tiny when it ran shorter than 16 times the thief’s running average of its steal cost (from the attempt until it holds the task). A fixed 500 ns let tasks of 1 µs be stolen, which bought nothing on AMD and on 4 sockets and 15% on Intel at nine times the CPU; with the factor 16 the threshold rose above 1 µs on all three server machines, as a fixed 2000 ns did. On the desktop processor, where steals are cheap, it stayed lower, and stealing the 1 µs tasks paid: that storm ran in 1307 instead of 2060 ns per step (on 7.4 instead of 1.1 cores). A higher threshold costs 4-10% on regions of 10000 tasks of 100 ns, and nothing in the suite.

Sleeping and waking

Sentinels. While some worker runs a task, two idle workers stay awake as sentinels: they look at the other workers’ deques in rounds, with 20 µs futex naps in between, and wake the others when they see work. A sentinel never steals itself: when it sees work it gives up its slot and steals as a normal thief, so that a sentinel always watches. Sentinels also take calls from outside Lace (lace_run_task) and end their nap at once when one arrives. A sentinel retires when no worker has run a task for the grace period.

Because a sentinel watches, sleeping workers can back off exponentially to 100 ms instead of waking every millisecond. Without sentinels, idle Lace used 8-35 times the CPU (0.21 instead of 0.025 CPU-s per 2 s on AMD, 0.43 instead of 0.025 on Intel, 0.52 instead of 0.015 on 4 sockets). On Intel sentinels also make regions after short idle periods faster: without them a tiny region after a 2 ms sleep took 20.7 instead of 9.5 µs, and a region of 1000 tasks 206 instead of 173 µs.

  • Two sentinels and a 200 µs spin before a sentinel’s first nap: with one sentinel and no spin, a tiny region 200 µs after the previous one took 5.9 instead of 2.1 µs on Intel and 3.1 instead of 1.4-1.9 µs on the desktop processor, and larger regions 2-7% longer on Intel; on AMD and on 4 sockets there was no difference.

  • Whether a sentinel asks a worker without shared tasks to share some made no difference. The 20 µs naps and the 1 µs timer slack that keeps them short matter on Intel: with 100 µs naps the tiny region after a 2 ms sleep took 16.7 instead of 9.5 µs, and without the slack 14.8 µs, with regions of 1000 tasks 24% slower. On AMD they made no clear difference.

  • On AMD, the suite ran 1.6-1.8% faster without sentinels (fft, lu, quicksort, strassen 5-9%), on Intel and on 4 sockets not. One sentinel, longer naps, not asking for sharing, the default timer slack, dropping the wake-up while no sentinel watches, and capping a sleeper’s timeout at 1 ms while some worker is busy all left it in place. The cause is not known; the design accepts the cost for the idle CPU, and on Intel the latency, that sentinels buy.

Shallow sleep. For 20 ms after it ran a task, a worker sleeps in naps of 100 µs, so its core stays in a shallow idle state and the next region starts fast. Against 5 ms, a region of 1000 tasks of 1 µs 5 ms after the previous one takes 168 instead of 227 µs (AMD), at less CPU per region, while an idle Lace uses 0.039 instead of 0.028 CPU-s per 2 s after work. The responsiveness was judged worth that.

Sentinel grace. A sentinel retires after 10 ms without work. 50 ms made tiny regions after short gaps faster while the helper wake-up still delayed them (3.0 instead of 5.1 µs on AMD), but with the helper woken by the caller (below) they are as fast with 10 ms, so the shorter grace stays.

Wake-ups.

  • A steal that leaves work at the victim wakes as many sleepers as the victim has tasks left (one futex call), instead of a chain of single wake-ups: regions of 1000-10000 tasks after a sleep start 10-14% faster.

  • A steal while no sentinel watches wakes a sleeper too (a region after a long idle, when nobody watches the work that the stolen root task spawns). Without it the AMD suite was 0.9% slower (fft, quicksort 7-10%).

  • The wake-up when a steal leaves work behind is needed besides the one when no sentinel watches: without it, regions of 1000 and 10000 tasks after a sleep took 6-9% longer on AMD.

  • A woken worker used to steal for 16 rounds before it slept again, first from the worker its waker named. With the sentinels and the wake-ups above, neither made a difference (one binary for all four combinations, on Intel and AMD; without the rounds, regions of 1000 tasks were 4-6% faster), so both were dropped.

Calls from outside Lace

The calling thread is not a worker: lace_run_task hands the task to a worker and waits.

  • The caller spins 5 µs, then yields now and then, and sleeps after 20 µs. Yielding helps when the caller shares a CPU with the worker that runs its task; yielding from the start cost short calls 20-28%.

  • A helper wake-up. The task may spawn work while all other workers sleep, so a second worker is woken. Without it lu and fft were up to 14% slower. But when the worker that took the task made that futex call, every tiny region took 2 µs longer (4.5 instead of 2.5 µs). In the design the waiting caller, which has nothing else to do, wakes the helper once it sees its task taken (or the worker does if the caller sleeps already), and a task that is done before the caller looks wakes nobody. This kept the throughput (without a helper the suite was 0.4-1.3% slower, lu up to 11%) and made tiny regions 200 µs after the previous one take 2.1-2.9 µs on the server machines and 1.4-1.9 µs on the desktop processor, as fast as without a helper.

  • Only the worker that ran a call stops backing off; the others keep backing off, so that a stream of calls does not keep every idle worker spinning.

Short calls take 2.8 µs on AMD, against 4.8 for OpenCilk and 6.1 for Parlay; 3.1 µs on Intel, against 3.4 and 3.9 (and 2.6 for v2.4.0, whose workers never stop spinning); and 2.0 µs on the desktop processor (Parlay 3.3). A tiny region after a 2 ms sleep takes 12.6 µs on AMD, against 2.3 for OpenCilk and 8.2 for Parlay, and 8.9 µs on Intel, against 5.0 and 4.5: the remaining cost of the handoff to a sleeping worker, which the design accepts. On the desktop processor it takes 3.8 µs, as with Parlay (3.6).

Pinning

Lace now pins its workers by default, within the affinity mask of the thread that calls lace_start (see Building for the modes and the API).

  • Unpinned costs 2-13% on every machine: up to 10% in the suite on 4 sockets (cholesky 1.88), 13% on PBBS on Intel when the mask includes the second hardware thread of each core (an unpinned worker or sentinel lands next to a busy worker). On AMD PBBS pinning made no difference.

  • One CPU per worker is fastest when the workers fill the cores of the mask, on every machine. Workers that may move lose their caches after a wake-up, and two of them sometimes share a core while another is idle. Groups (one L3 cache, one NUMA node) were 1-4% slower than one CPU per worker on Intel, where an L3 group is a socket, and as fast on AMD, where it is a 4-core chiplet.

  • One CPU per worker under interference. With busy loops on 4 of 16 cores, one CPU per worker slowed down least (1.18 at 16 workers and 1.46 at 8, against 1.22 and 1.63 for v2.4.0): the workers on free cores keep running and take the work of those that share a core.

  • But not with spare cores. Two programs that each pin worker i to the i-th core pile onto the same cores: two copies took 1.95 times as long as one, against 1.14 for v2.4.0. With fewer workers than cores, the default therefore lets every worker run on any core of the mask (1.14).

The default (LACE_PIN_AUTO) is the best or within 1.4% of the best mode in every test.

OpenCilk’s release runtime crashes on uts T3L: it caps fiber stacks at 8 MB, and more than 1024 nested spawns write past its deque into the heap. With a patched runtime (1 GiB stacks, a deque of 99999 frames) it runs, and took 1.02-1.06 times as long as Lace at 8 and 16 workers on AMD and Intel. The patched runtime is as fast as the release on the other programs (Intel suite within 2%).

At 8 workers on AMD Lace took 0.996 and OpenCilk 0.957. The two machines differ:

  • On Intel Lace was faster than OpenCilk on 13 of the 21 benchmarks at 16 workers (0.995 of its time; an earlier round with the previous helper rule, 18 of 21). At 8 workers one run gave Lace 1.042 of Parlay’s time; a second run did not reproduce that (Lace 0.980, OpenCilk 0.961), and pinning one CPU per worker (0.989) or per socket (0.984) instead of leaving the 8 workers unpinned on the 18 cores made no difference. OpenCilk was faster than Lace on 12 of the 21 benchmarks at 8 workers (Lace 1.035 of its time); Lace’s largest gaps to Parlay on Intel are delaunayRefine and delaunayTriangulation (1.08-1.21) and rangeQuery2d.

  • On AMD OpenCilk was faster than Lace on 18 of the 21 benchmarks (Lace 1.054 of its time, 1.041 without maximalMatching, where OpenCilk took 0.71 of Parlay’s time). Lace’s lead over Parlay there comes from three benchmarks where Parlay’s scheduler does poorly (classify, convexHull, rayCast: Lace and OpenCilk 0.75-0.88 of Parlay’s time); without them Lace and Parlay are equal (0.993).

OpenCilk crashes on two BFS inputs in some runs.

A diagnosis on AMD looked at the seven benchmarks where Lace or OpenCilk stood out (2 rounds; the geomeans below are over these seven, not the whole set). It separated three causes:

  • Not pinning: Lace unpinned, pinned per CPU, and on one hardware thread per core was within 1%.

  • OpenCilk’s lead on maximalMatching (0.76 of Parlay’s time) comes from ``cilk_for``, not from its scheduler: ParlayLib’s OpenCilk backend turns a loop into a cilk_for, while Parlay and the Lace plugin pick a grain with a sequential prefix and split in halves. With ParlayLib’s OpenCilk backend changed to split loops as Parlay does, OpenCilk took 1.02 of Parlay’s time there (Lace 1.00), and the difference sits in the phase whose loops become a cilk_for. Picking the grain as cilk_for does (at most 2048 iterations or 1/8P of the range) did not help Lace (1.054 against 1.040 on the seven), so it is the compiled loop, which a library cannot offer. Hardware counters show no locality advantage: OpenCilk had as many fills from other chiplets as Parlay (1.06; on maximalMatching 1.17) and Lace 0.96, and on maximalMatching it ran the same instructions in 0.90 of the cycles. (Parlay’s scheduler did four times as many cross-chiplet fills on classify as Lace and OpenCilk, which is why it is slow there.)

  • Lace’s own gap to Parlay on these benchmarks (1.04 on the seven, 4-9% on histogram, removeDuplicates and the graph benchmarks) comes from waiting at joins. With random leaps back on it shrank to 1.014 (removeDuplicates 1.066 to 1.010, maximalMatching 1.025 to 0.985): Parlay’s scheduler, too, runs any task while it waits. Random leaps cost the suite 8-11% and Sylvan up to 36%, and random leaps only while no worker is idle gave the same 1.015 here and did not help the suite; by the time a waiting worker knows that a random leap would have paid, the chance is gone. The design keeps leapfrogging only and accepts this gap. The PIE times show where it goes: without random leaps, workers spend 2-4% of their time searching for work at a join on these benchmarks (1% on sampleSort); with random leaps 0.5-2%, the work done while waiting rises from 8% to 30-45% of their time, and they make 3-4 times fewer leap attempts.

  • rangeQuery2d (1.07-1.09) did not change with random leaps; its build phase takes twice as long as with Parlay or OpenCilk, for a reason not yet known.

OpenCilk scales as well as Lace on the coarse-grained programs (cilksort, lu, rectmul, strassen, uts; slightly better on uts T3L), and its spawn cost limits it on the fine-grained ones (fib 2.9, knapsack 5.5, pi 4.0 at 32 workers). heat and quicksort stop at 6-8 with all three.

Two programs scale worse than with v2.4.0. uts T3L reaches a speedup of 30 against 32 (OpenCilk 33), and random leaps make it 1.30 times as fast. quicksort takes 1.24 against 0.99 s (OpenCilk 1.09); neither leap setting changes that, and on the 32-core AMD node it took 4-9% longer than with v2.4.0 too. With 64 workers waking the sleepers costs more: a region of 1000 tasks of 1 µs after a 5 ms sleep takes 172 µs (v2.4.0 1099, OpenCilk 68, Parlay 48), and one of 100000 tasks of 100 ns 558 µs (4944, 319, 360). An idle Lace uses 0.074 CPU-s in 2 s after work, v2.4.0 1.87.

Tried and dropped

  • Random leaps, and random leaps only while all workers are busy (they would close most of the gap to Parlay on PBBS, at a larger cost elsewhere).

  • A cilk_for-style grain in the Parlay plugin (no gain).

  • A fixed tiny-steal threshold (2000 ns was as good on the server machines, but gave up the gain from cheap steals on the desktop processor).

  • A helper wake-up by the worker that takes the task, also only when all other workers sleep (it did not remove the 2 µs).

  • One sentinel without the spin, and a sentinel grace of 50 ms.

  • A cap of 1 ms on a sleeper’s timeout while some worker is busy (no effect on any machine).

  • Pinning blocks of cores per worker when there are few workers (lost to all cores per worker with two programs).

  • A 100 µs sentinel nap (slower on Intel), sentinels that do not ask for sharing (no difference).

  • Extra steal rounds after a wake-up, and a hint which worker to steal from first (no difference once sentinels watch).

Open issues

  • The handoff to a sleeping worker after a few milliseconds of idle (9-13 µs against OpenCilk’s 2.3-5), the price of a calling thread that is not a worker.

  • PBBS on AMD: OpenCilk is ahead of Lace on 18 of 21 benchmarks (4% without maximalMatching, whose gap is cilk_for). With Parlay’s loops OpenCilk is still 3-8% ahead on histogram, maximalIndependentSet and wordCounts: it never waits at a join (continuation stealing, out of reach for Lace with one stack per worker), and the hardware counters show no locality advantage. On a few benchmarks Lace is 4-9% behind Parlay, the price of leapfrogging only. The build phase of rangeQuery2d is slow on every machine.

  • On AMD EPYC the suite runs 1.6-1.8% faster without sentinels; none of the sentinel variants tried removed that.

  • At 64 cores (see Scaling): uts T3L, which random leaps would help at a larger cost elsewhere, and quicksort on AMD, whose cause is not known. Regions after a few milliseconds of idle start 2.5-3.6 times slower than with OpenCilk or Parlay.

  • Pinning on FreeBSD and Windows (Linux only so far); timer granularity on Windows and macOS would change the idle behaviour, which was measured on Linux only.

  • Scaling beyond 64 cores was not tested.
